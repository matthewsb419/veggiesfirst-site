You stop doomscrolling by changing the phone, not by trying harder. Give the apps you scroll a daily time allowance, turn off the notifications that pull you back in, and decide what has to be done before you open them. Willpower runs out at 11 PM. A setting you made at noon doesn't.

The six changes below take about fifteen minutes. The iPhone steps were checked against Apple's support pages for iOS 27.

## What doomscrolling is, and why it's hard to stop

University of Florida researchers who built a way to measure it describe doomscrolling as bingeing on negative news. Most people now use the word more loosely, for any scrolling that carries on long after it stopped being useful or fun.

It's hard to stop because a feed has no end. A book has chapters and a show has episodes. A feed gives you nothing that says "this is a good place to put it down", so you have to supply the stopping point yourself. Every change below does one of two things: it puts a pause in front of opening the app, or it builds in a point where the session ends.

The research on what doomscrolling does to people is still thin. A 2024 study of 800 university students in Iran and the United States found that those who doomscrolled more also reported more worry about big questions of life and death. The link was moderate and the results were correlational, so the study doesn't show which one causes the other. The University of Florida team said the same about their own work. You don't need the science settled to want your evening back.

## 1. Give the apps you scroll a daily time allowance

Screen Time is built into the iPhone. In iOS 27 it sets limits by category, such as Social Media, Entertainment and Games.

1. Open Settings and tap Screen Time.
2. Tap Time Allowances. If Screen Time isn't on yet, tap Turn On App & Website Activity.
3. Drag the slider to set how much time you get.
4. Tap Manage Categories to add or change the categories that have an allowance.

Apple's page says you can use the apps in a category until they reach either the category's allowance or your daily total, whichever comes first.

Pick a number you can keep. If you scroll three hours a day now, a 30 minute allowance will feel like a punishment and you'll switch it off by Thursday. Start at about two thirds of what you use today and bring it down each week.

On iOS 26 and earlier this part of Screen Time has different names, so the steps above won't match what you see.

## 2. Turn off the notifications that pull you back

A lot of scrolling starts with a notification that had nothing to do with you: a suggested post, a "trending now", a nudge that someone you barely know has posted.

1. Open Settings and tap Notifications.
2. Tap the app.
3. Turn off Allow Notifications.

If you still want to hear from an app, only not all day, use Scheduled Summary instead. In Settings, tap Notifications, then Scheduled Summary. Turn it on, set the times you want the summary to arrive, and choose the apps that go in it. Their notifications then turn up together at those times and stay quiet in between.

## 3. Make the screen gray

Feeds are built out of color: red badges, bright thumbnails, video. With the color gone, the same feed is duller, and it's easier to put down.

1. Open Settings and go to Accessibility, then Display & Text Size.
2. Tap Color Filters and turn on Color Filters.
3. Pick the grayscale filter.

Try it for a week before you judge it. Photos and maps look worse, which is the trade.

## 4. Do one thing first

A time limit says how long you can scroll. It says nothing about when. If scrolling is the first thing you do in the morning or the thing you do when a job feels too big, the limit gets used up on avoiding the job.

So put something in front of it. Before the first scroll of the day, finish one thing that's on your list: the dishes, the email you've been putting off, twenty minutes of the assignment. Scrolling after that is a break. Scrolling before it is a way of not starting.

Write the list the night before. In the morning you won't feel like deciding what matters, and "I'll work out what to do after I check my phone" is how the morning goes.

## 5. Decide where the session ends before it starts

Since the feed won't end, pick the end yourself before you open the app. Ten minutes on a timer. Until the kettle boils. Until the bus comes. A stopping point you chose with a clear head is much easier to keep than one you try to find in the middle of a video.

When the timer goes, close the app and stand up. Moving breaks the loop better than promising yourself one more.

## 6. Keep the phone out of reach at the two worst times

Bed is where a quick look most easily turns into an hour: last thing at night and first thing in the morning. Charge the phone somewhere you'd have to get up to reach. If it's your alarm, a cheap alarm clock removes the excuse.

You don't have to do this all day. Those two times are enough to start with.

## What if you just ignore the limits?

It happens. Any rule you can switch off in two taps is only as strong as you are in that moment, and that moment is when you're tired and the phone is in your hand.

Three things help:

- **Change the rules for tomorrow, not tonight.** If the allowance feels wrong, wait until morning to adjust it. A rule you loosen in the moment isn't a rule.
- **Tell someone.** Saying "I'm off my phone by ten" to a person you live with makes it a little harder to break.
- **Tie the scrolling to something you've done.** This is change 4, and it holds up best, because the apps open after you finish something. They aren't simply taken away.

## How Veggies First does it

Veggies First is an iPhone to-do list that does change 4 for you. Your to-dos for the day are "veggies". The apps you scroll are "dessert": you pick single apps, whole categories or websites. While a veggie is left on today's list, a dessert app shows a lock screen instead of opening, and the lock screen lists what's left to do.

![The lock screen on a dessert app: locked until you finish a veggie, with today's veggies listed.](/img/02-locked.jpg)

Finish a veggie and you earn coins. One coin buys one minute in a dessert app, counted as minutes you actually use. Dessert comes in sessions: when a session is used up, dessert takes a short break before the next one can start, so the feed gets the stopping point it doesn't have by itself. Clear the whole list and dessert is open for the rest of the day.

It's built to be hard to get around in the moment. Changes to how much a veggie earns, how long a session is and how long the break is all start at midnight. An app you take off the dessert list stays locked until your list is clear or midnight, whichever comes first. A day with nothing on the list starts locked until you add a veggie or say on the lock screen that you have nothing to do.

It isn't a ban. There are a few passes, which open dessert without finishing a veggie, for the days you need one. Using a pass takes a deliberate hold, and you can't use two in a row without finishing a veggie in between.

It uses Apple's Screen Time to lock apps, so it's for iPhone only, and it never sees what you do inside an app. Screen Time only reports minutes of use.

## Where to start

If you do one thing today, set the time allowance. It takes two minutes. If you do two, write tomorrow's list tonight and don't open a feed tomorrow until the first thing on it is done.
