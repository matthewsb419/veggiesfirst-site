Most to-do lists are really wish lists: twenty items, half of them vague, the same ones carried over all week. A list you finish is short and specific, and it has a reason to be finished. Five rules get you there.

## 1. Write it the night before

You know what's left and what's coming, and you aren't tired of tomorrow yet. In Veggies First an evening review walks you through it. Each leftover from today gets a decision, then you confirm tomorrow's plate.

## 2. Keep it short enough to clear

The aim is to finish, so plan what one day can hold. In Veggies First there's a strong reason to: a clean plate opens the apps you've locked for the rest of the day. Three to five real things beats twelve hopeful ones.

![A plate in Veggies First: a short list for the day, focus items first.](/img/01-plate.jpg)

## 3. Make each one a thing you can do

"Sort out the garage" will sit there all week. "Fill one bag for donation" gets done. If a to-do has been waiting for days, it's too big or too vague. The app shows how old each one is, which is an honest way to spot them.

## 4. Give the rest somewhere to go

A list stays short only if the things that aren't for today have a home. That's the backlog: everything you'll get to someday, one tap away from tomorrow's plate. A calendar takes to-dos for any day ahead. Repeating to-dos put themselves on the list, so you don't have to rewrite them.

## 5. Decide what happens to leftovers

A leftover that rolls over on its own teaches you the list doesn't matter. In Veggies First, yesterday's unfinished to-dos come back with a question: do it today, move it to tomorrow, put it in the backlog, or let it go. "Let it go" is allowed, and some things should be.

## And give it teeth

Rules one to five are good advice that's easy to ignore. What makes the list get finished is having something depend on it.

In Veggies First, the apps you scroll are locked while to-dos are left, and each one you finish earns minutes. The list stops being a note to yourself and becomes what stands between you and your evening.

It's set up so the list can't quietly shrink either. Deleting a to-do you're not getting to counts against the day. A real mistake, like something typed twice, comes off without counting, a few times a day. You can't move things to tomorrow until the evening. An empty list starts the day locked.

## What a good list looks like

- one thing you're dreading, at the top
- two or three ordinary jobs
- one small habit that repeats

That's five. Do them and you're finished for the day.
